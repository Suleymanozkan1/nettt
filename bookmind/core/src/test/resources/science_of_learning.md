## The Forgetting Curve

The forgetting curve is a model that shows how quickly learned information fades over time. In the late nineteenth century Hermann Ebbinghaus measured his own memory by memorising lists of nonsense syllables. Ebbinghaus observed that most of the information was lost in the first hours after learning, and that the loss then slowed down.

The meaning of this observation in daily life is clear. On the day we finish a book we feel that we remember its content well; a week later, only a few main ideas are left. The forgetting curve shows that this loss is not random but follows a regular pattern.

Forgetting does not happen at the same speed for everyone. Meaningful information, links to what we already know and the emotional importance of a topic all slow forgetting down. In experiments with nonsense syllables the curve is very steep; with a meaningful story it is much flatter.

The most effective way to flatten the forgetting curve is to review information at the right time. Every successful review extends how long the information stays in memory. This is why the forgetting curve is the foundation of spaced repetition.

## Spaced Repetition

Spaced repetition is a learning method based on studying information again at gradually increasing intervals. A concept is reviewed on the first day, then three days later, then a week later and then a month later. Each review catches the information just as it is about to be forgotten.

The difference between massed and spaced study has been measured in many experiments. Intensive studying the night before an exam is called massed practice. Massed practice seems to work in the short term, but a few weeks later people remember clearly less than after spaced study.

Modern spaced repetition algorithms keep a separate memory model for every piece of information. Stability is the time it takes for the probability of recalling an item to fall to ninety percent. Difficulty is a value that shows how easily an item is consolidated. After every answer the algorithm updates these two values and calculates the next review date.

The success of spaced repetition depends on reviewing regularly. A missed day does not break the whole system, though. A late review is a little harder, but when it succeeds it strengthens the information even more.

## Active Recall

Active recall is the practice of retrieving information from memory instead of reading it again. Rereading a paragraph creates a feeling of familiarity; but familiarity does not mean that we can actually recall the information. This mistake is called the fluency illusion.

The testing effect is the finding that trying to remember strengthens learning by itself. When students read a text once and then test themselves instead of reading it twice, they remember much more a week later. The effort spent while recalling strengthens the memory trace.

Active recall has different levels. Recognition is the act of choosing the right answer among options. Free recall is the act of producing the answer without any cue. Explanation is the act of describing a concept in our own words. Recognition is the easiest level; explanation is the strongest sign of understanding.

Self-explanation is the habit of putting an idea into words as if we were teaching it to someone else. This method makes gaps in our knowledge visible. At the point where we cannot explain a concept, we notice that we have not fully understood it yet.

## Cognitive Load

Cognitive load is the amount of mental effort that working memory carries at a given moment. Working memory can handle only a limited number of items at the same time. When this limit is exceeded, learning slows down and mistakes increase.

Intrinsic load is the load that comes from the complexity of the topic itself. Extraneous load is the load created by poorly designed explanations, unnecessary detail and cluttered presentation. Good instructional design reduces extraneous load and frees mental resources for the actual learning.

Breaking a complex topic into small parts reduces cognitive load. Basic concepts are learned first, and then they are combined. A learner who lacks prerequisite knowledge struggles with a new topic, because every new piece of information takes up a separate slot in working memory.

Studying examples also reduces cognitive load. A worked example is an example that shows step by step how a problem is solved. For beginners, studying worked examples is more efficient than trying to solve the problem from scratch.

## Transfer

Transfer is the ability to use knowledge learned in one context in a new context. If someone who learned about inflation in an economics book can use that knowledge to interpret changing supermarket prices, transfer has taken place.

Near transfer is the use of knowledge in a situation that closely resembles the one in which it was learned. Far transfer is the application of knowledge in a quite different field. Far transfer is hard and rarely happens on its own.

The way to make transfer easier is to study a concept with several examples and in different contexts. A concept learned with a single example stays tied to that example. Different examples help to separate the essence of the concept from the examples.

Real-life practice tasks are the strongest test of transfer. Trying to use a learned principle in everyday decisions for a week deepens both remembering and understanding of that principle. Knowledge only counts as truly learned when it is used.
